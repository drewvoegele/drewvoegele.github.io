Chapter 032019–2021

Accelerating a leader

Power BI led its market, but its growth was slowing. Satisfaction rose more than 40%, and the work revealed the product's two audiences, a question that led to Fabric.

Role
Design Studio Lead, Power BI
Impact
Raised Power BI's Net Promoter Score by more than 40% in two years while adoption kept climbing.
01

The context

Until 2019, my work had been taking an idea from nothing to its first version. Coming back to Power BI was a different job. I inherited a mature product, the market leader, in the middle of its own growing pains. Satisfaction and growth in new users were slowing. Too many people opened it and left before doing anything useful. It was like opening a music app and never playing a song.

02

Data in space

I brought mixed reality with me. HoloLens was expensive and hard to get into people's hands, but everyone had a phone. So with the Power BI mobile team, we put data in the room through the phone's camera. Hold the phone where you want a report, press Pin in space, and walk away. It stays there for the next person who scans the room, and a radar points you to any pinned report with an alert. It shipped in 2020.

03

Habituation

We were giving Power BI a new look, to make it easier to use and to give the content more room. Most people arrived through a link to one specific report, and in Power BI apps we wanted the content to shine, not the shell around it, which could pull people away from what they came to read. User tests said the changes helped. Then our Net Promoter Score dipped at the two-week mark.

People dislike change even when it's better. A new interface dips before it rises. So we watched the score longer, especially while people could still switch the new look off, and judged changes at four to eight weeks against the expected adaptation curve instead of a two-week snapshot.

The two-week numbers said roll it back. We waited for people to get used to it again, the curve turned at about a month, and that design is how apps work in Power BI today.

04

Two audiences

Soon after I came back to Power BI, I visited our team in Israel. They were about to ship deployment pipelines: dev, test and production stages for your reports. That's critical for developers. But most of our users weren't developers. They made reports. They'd click on it and have no idea why it was there.

So the team tried rules: only show it to workspace admins. But everyone had workspaces. And creating a team in Microsoft Teams quietly created a Power BI workspace too, which turned on the developer tools. Starting a Teams channel was enough to put developer tooling in front of someone who just wanted a report.

That's when it clicked: we were serving two different people with one product. Rules about who sees what would never be good enough. I started to think the consumer and the developer each needed their own experience.

05

Adjacent markets

We moved into nearby markets. We shipped Datamarts, a simple way for business users to build their own data warehouse. We mapped the user journey with the product manager and customers. Then my design team and I created the structure, interactions and pixels ourselves. We also explored other developer-type experiences that never shipped. All of it pointed the same way: developers needed an experience of their own, one that supported the whole data estate end to end.

Every move like this pulled the product further from the person who just wanted a report. It got clearer to me, and to others, including some of our leaders, that developer experiences couldn't keep living inside the consumer experience.

06

One org, then one platform

In 2021, Power BI's design team merged with Azure Data's, and the larger org kept the Azure Data name. Then we started on what became Fabric. The design team, along with many PMs and engineers, saw it as the chance to give developers a home of their own. That's the next chapter.
